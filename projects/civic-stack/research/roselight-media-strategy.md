# Rose Light + Recovery Tracker: Local-Media Strategy Analysis

## 1. Editorial identity: from aggregator to instrument

Rose Light today is a commodity — 22 RSS feeds, AI scoring, a digest. Anyone with cron and an API key can clone it, and readers can't tell you from a feed reader. The Recovery Tracker changes the category Rose Light competes in. Aggregators summarize what others report; the Tracker creates **original structured data that doesn't exist anywhere else** (13 vectors with status/owner/next-step, plus a 16-address vacancy inventory west of SW 10th that no agency publishes). That's the Block Club/BikePortland lesson: the durable local outlets own a *beat* and a *dataset*, not a firehose. The identity shift is from "light on what matters" (curation claim) to "the scoreboard for downtown recovery" (accountability claim). The aggregation layer stops being the product and becomes the *sensing apparatus that keeps the scoreboard current* — which is exactly the framing GZ already built (sensing → sense-making → action), just turned outward as editorial positioning.

## 2. The growth loop the tracker unlocks

An aggregator's loop is weak: read → maybe subscribe → churn. The tracker creates a compounding loop:

1. **Vector pages are permanent, linkable, rankable assets.** "James Beard Public Market status" or "Burnside Bridge timeline" are evergreen search queries with no canonical answer page. Each vector page, fed by its tag stream, becomes the reference URL reporters, councilors, and neighbors link to.
2. **Every status change is a story.** Aggregators wait for news; a tracker *generates* news ("Vector 7 moved from stalled to moving"). That's a native reason to email subscribers beyond "here's this week's links."
3. **Citizen next-steps close the loop.** Reader → action → outcome → status change → digest item. This is what Axios Local never does and what makes readers feel ownership rather than consumption.
4. **The Burnside site's signup piping into Subscriber with `source="betterburnside"` means the campaign is an audience-acquisition channel for the tracker**, and the tracker legitimizes the campaign. That's the flywheel in miniature.

## 3. Making the digest the civic brief of record

"Of record" is earned by three properties: **regularity** (never miss a week — the streak is the product), **structure** (a fixed, scannable rubric: vector movements, watcher sections on EQRB funding/council agendas/corridor closures, one vacancy-inventory update), and **being cited**. The 12-councilor structure is the wedge: D4 staffers are new, understaffed, and starving for constituent signal. A digest that reliably answers "what moved downtown this week and who owns it" is a staff-briefing substitute. Target being *forwarded inside* the D4 office, the DNA/Pearl/Old Town neighborhood orgs, and one newsroom Slack. The moment a WW or BikePortland piece cites "according to the Portland Recovery Tracker," record status is effectively conferred.

## 4. Earned-media moments, ranked

- **The 12-years-vacant Matador** is the lead. It's concrete, photographable, emotionally legible, and timed perfectly against the April 2026 council vacancy-tax debate and the Old Town "No Vacancy" pilot. Pitch: "A citizen mapped 16 vacant storefronts on West Burnside. One has been empty since 2014." WW or the Mercury takes that.
- **The no-data-west-of-10th gap** is the credibility story, best for OPB or a policy-adjacent outlet: "The city debates a vacancy tax with no vacancy data for the stadium district — so a resident built the dataset." It positions the tracker as filling a government gap, which is the strongest possible frame for a citizen data project.
- **The bridge-delay reframe** ("indefinite postponement = prep years") is an op-ed/BikePortland essay, not a news pitch — it's an argument, and it's better delivered *after* the tracker has established GZ as the data person, so the opinion arrives with earned standing. Sequence: Matador → data-gap → reframe.

## 5. The dual-role problem: publisher vs. campaigner

This is the real credibility risk. If the "neutral scoreboard" is run by the guy leading the Burnside campaign, institutions will discount both. Handle it with **disclosure and separation, not pretense of neutrality**:

- Persistent disclosure on the tracker and in every digest: "Rose Light's editor also organizes on West Burnside (betterburnside link)." Never let a reporter discover it.
- **Methodology page**: how statuses are set, sourcing, the verify-before-publish trust model, correction policy. Field-verified + cited beats "neutral" — BikePortland is openly pro-bike and is still the outlet of record on its beat because its facts hold.
- Keep hard separation of *voice*: tracker/digest states facts and status; advocacy lives on the Burnside site and in op-eds under GZ's name. Never editorialize inside a vector page.
- Fastest fix long-term: the coalition (once named) becomes the campaign's face, and GZ recedes to "publisher + member."

## 6. The number that makes institutions care

Raw subscriber count matters less than composition. Benchmarks: **~1,000 true local subscribers with 45%+ open rate** is where a hyperlocal newsletter becomes citable; that's achievable in 12 months solo. But the real threshold is smaller: **50–100 named institutional readers** — D4 council staff, PBOT/Prosper Portland comms, neighborhood-association chairs, 3–5 reporters — verifiably opening weekly. Track it via the source field and open data; when a councilor's office *replies* to a digest, you're the brief of record regardless of list size. State the goal internally as "1,000 doors, 100 desks."
